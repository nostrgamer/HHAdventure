It's been almost 2 months since my last update and below are some notes of my proposed "design", based on watching several other van builds trim out their interiors. To say that this list was an oversimplification of what needed to be done would be understating things dramatically:

1) Remove excess fiberglass trim, as appropriate.
2) Build 3/4" interior box top and sides, adhere with Sikaflex and self-tapping screws.
3) Measure and fit bottom of box to match the top of bed.
4) Add outer box sides from 3/4" wood, attach to bottom of interior box. Upper attaches to hard points above.
5) Add upper outer box from 3/4" wood, adjust height to allow for upper sill to be angled at 45 degrees (or to fit).
6) Add insulation with 3M 90 directly to fiberglass.
7) Cut 1/4" plywood to fit window and approximate your size box (window cut out).
8) Attach 1/2" siding panels to adhere the entire box in place.
9) Attach 1/4" window trim pieces on all 4 sides.
10) Router out 1/4" plywood wall panel based on the window box you've now constructed.
11) Upholster plywood window cutout as well as all 1/4" plywood window pieces.
12) Re-assemble and tack down plywood wall panel.

It should be noted that even for most DIY builders, doing the full trim of your van capsule interior is unusual. There are only a handful of videos on the internet, most of them done by professional builders. Why? Because, for about $1,500 you can just buy your trim interiors, custom CNC machined for your particular van capsule. So, why did we not do this? Because I am a bit cheap and a crazy person. Also, I didn't quite like the look of even the pre-built systems.

![The pre-built trim option we passed on](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/PurchasedTrim.jpg)

Before starting, I spent an embarrassingly long time staring at this and trying to determine exactly how I wanted to build this out.

![Staring at the capsule, planning the build](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0493.jpg)

We had already locked in our bed height to accommodate our refrigerator, so it could not be moved lower. Additionally, I noticed that while on our 10" bed (including topper), I would nearly hit my head on the jagged metal above when getting out of bed. So, I had to figure out how to not lose any space above us as well as work with the most uneven and unusual geometries of the van. Not only is the capsule not rectangular, but the depth of the wall changes both vertically and horizontally.

So first, I needed to make a box; this was something I was able to get some good direction from on YouTube.

![Assembling the window frame box](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0495.jpg)

I built three sides of the box and adhered it to the frame of the van utilizing self-tapping metal screws.

![Box adhered with self-tapping screws](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0496.jpg)

Once this was fixed and I knew I wouldn't be changing it, I came back and reinforced this with an adhesive to give it extra strength. The pocket jig got a workout from this point on in the build, definitely winning the MVP award.

![The pocket jig, MVP of the build](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0497.jpg)

For this part of the build, I finished both sides, since I knew this was definitely how I wanted the framing.

![Both sides of the box framing done](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0499.jpg)

Next up, I reinforced the lower wall cladding that originally was 1/2" plywood, replacing it with 3/4" to give it extra strength and allow a 3/4" vertical stringer to be attached. See the right red arrow below. I then added another 3/4" vertical stringer (left red arrow) to the rear of the van which would allow the wall to attach to it.

![Reinforced lower wall cladding and vertical stringers](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0500.jpg)

![Red arrows marking the stringers, blue arrows showing the differing planes](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0501.jpg)

![More of the differing planes across the capsule](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0502.jpg)

Where I have my main wall stringers is on a different plane than the window box frame (shown by the red arrows). You can see the various differing planes shown with the blue arrows. This meant that I would need to build my window box into the van to conform to the plane of the walls. This was required because the original metal van walls are joined by metal columns along key structural parts of the van wall. Therefore, the wooden interior walls I would construct need to come into the van to meet these columns. I repeated this process on the other side of the van, and below you can see I built 3/4" stub outs from the window boxes which conform to the same plane as the vertical stringers as well as a horizontal stringer I adhered to the upper horizontal wall stringer. This would eventually allow me to place one piece of 1/4" plywood across all of these areas and have it attach on the same plane.

![3/4-inch stub outs conforming to the stringer plane](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0538.jpg)

Next, I cut out a 1/4" piece of plywood that would serve as the window cut out, covering both the fiberglass, as well as squaring off the van capsule so that the vertical and horizontal skew did not interfere with a 90 degree joint of the side of my window box. Additionally, it would allow me to insulate behind the panel, making the cabin more comfortable. My wife found the design for the window cutout online, which I printed onto several standard pieces of paper and then cut to size. This provided a stencil I could use to cut out the window from the 1/4" plywood.

![Paper stencil for the window cutout](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0510.jpg)

![Cutting the window from the 1/4-inch plywood](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0560.jpg)

Fitting this into the capsule then allowed me to build the walls of the window box as well as the lower and upper sill, each of which was cut from 1/2" plywood.

![Window cutout fitted into the capsule](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0563.jpg)

![Building the walls of the window box](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0564.jpg)

![Lower and upper sills from 1/2-inch plywood](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0515.jpg)

On top of this plywood, I cut out 1/4" plywood, which would be tacked on top. Each of these pieces had to be labeled, including their orientation in the van so that they could easily be removed for upholstery and then reinstalled correctly.

![1/4-inch pieces tacked on top](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0516.jpg)

![Every piece labeled with its orientation](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0571.jpg)

The 1/2" plywood walls, and lower and upper sill did not need to be as precisely cut because they would be covered with 1/4" plywood. The 1/4" plywood was scribed more carefully using a compass to get the exact fit.

![Scribing the 1/4-inch plywood with a compass](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0568.jpg)

![Exact fit after scribing](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0519.jpg)

These 1/4" pieces were designed to be covered with fabric later on and were therefore cut about 1/16" short on all sides to allow for covering, while not overlapping each other or the exterior wall. The blue arrows show the 1/4" cover pieces, the orange arrow shows the 1/2" wall pieces, and the red arrow shows the underlying structural 3/4" wall piece.

![Blue arrows: 1/4-inch covers. Orange: 1/2-inch wall. Red: 3/4-inch structural piece](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0521.jpg)

Next up was the most intimidating part of the build — routing the 1/4" exterior wall panel so that the interior window cutout would precisely track what I had built thus far. Here is a shot doing some test runs with the router.

![Router test runs](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0523.jpg)

Additionally, it was at this time that I learned about the various types of router bits that are available and what I needed was a 1/4" laminate/flush trim routing bit.

![1/4-inch laminate/flush trim bit](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0524.jpg)

Of course routing makes quite a considerable mess, so I prepared the van with a drop cloth before I got started.

![Drop cloth down before the mess](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0525.jpg)

One of the tricks to this method is making sure you have the 1/4" board firmly pressed up against the wall cutout and opening the window behind the panel can help you ensure you have it fully seated.

![Panel firmly seated against the cutout](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0526.jpg)

I first started by drilling out a hole and then began to route the contour.

![Starting hole drilled](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0527.jpg)

![Routing the contour](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0528.jpg)

![Routing continued](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0529.jpg)

While nerve wracking, the end result turned out well I think.

![The routed wall panel](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0530.jpg)

Next up was covering all the 1/4" cover pieces and window cutout with fabric — shown here is the first piece being prepared and finished.

![First piece being wrapped in fabric](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0546.jpg)

![First piece finished](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0549.jpg)

In addition to fabric, I ended up gluing insulation behind the window cutout.

![Insulation glued behind the window cutout](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0550.jpg)

The first few pieces set into the window with accompanying wall insulation added.

![First pieces installed with wall insulation](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0553.jpg)

![Fitting a top piece with the wall cutout](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0555.jpg)

Finally, I added a 3/4" support board below the lower sill to provide additional rigidity to the window box.

![3/4-inch support board below the lower sill](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0557.jpg)

While I was upholstering the other wall, I also disassembled both window box wall cutouts to add clear coat so they matched the ceiling.

![Clear coat on the window box cutouts](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0581.jpg)

Here are the passenger wall upholstered pieces. The extremely detailed work on the window cutout piece was done by my talented wife, since it took a lot of patience to ensure that none of the fabric bunched in the corners.

![Passenger wall upholstered pieces](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0579.jpg)

The passenger wall went together much faster than the driver side, simply because I knew exactly how to build it the second time round.

![Passenger wall coming together](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0582.jpg)

One of the final components was to build a frame piece that went across the van and would prevent the bed from moving forward during transit. This piece was cut from three sections and has a cutout to make getting into the bed easier but prevents the top of the bed from moving.

![Bed retainer frame across the van](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0583.jpg)

The window box bottom was cut 5" from the top of the bed slats which meant that our 5" full size foam mattress fit perfectly.

![5-inch mattress fitting perfectly in the window box](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0591.jpg)

The final product, shown here includes the 3" topper (which was cut out in each of the 4 corners) to prevent bunching.

![Final product with 3-inch topper](/images/Van%20Capsule%20Interior/IMG_0592.jpg)

While this has been quite a long part of the van build, I'm very proud of the end result as it demonstrates how much I've learned working with wood this year. I'm looking forward to the next project, which will be a new kitchen.
